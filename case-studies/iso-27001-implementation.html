Case study · ISO/IEC 27001 implementation

Getting a SaaS company to ISO 27001 without drowning it in paperwork

How I would scope, assess and evidence an ISMS for a growing company whose bank customers want certification within a year.

Sample work. The company and all figures below are fictional and do not describe any client engagement. The scenario reflects common situations in this type of project.

Company
Tessaro Cloud (fictional), B2B SaaS, 70 staff
Hosting
Public cloud, fully remote engineering
Driver
Bank customers require ISO 27001 in their due diligence
Constraint
No dedicated security team, 12 months, sceptical engineers

01 · The situation

What the company was facing

Two financial services prospects told Tessaro Cloud that ISO 27001 certification would be a condition for signing. The company had good engineering habits, but nothing written down: no risk assessment, scattered policies, and security knowledge concentrated in two senior engineers.

The real risk was not failing the audit. It was building an ISMS that passes once and then decays, because nobody in engineering sees it as part of their work.

02 · Key decisions

Five decisions that shaped the project

  1. Scope the ISMS around the product, not the whole company

    Decision: The scope covers the SaaS platform and the functions that support it: engineering, cloud operations, customer support, HR onboarding and offboarding, and supplier management. Interfaces with out-of-scope areas, such as marketing systems, are documented as required by clause 4.3.

    Why: This is what customers actually rely on, and it keeps the first certification achievable within 12 months.

    Trade-off: A narrower scope must be explained clearly to customers, so the scope statement is written in plain language for due diligence questionnaires.

  2. Use scenario-based risk assessment instead of a huge asset list

    Decision: Around 40 risk scenarios (for example, "leaked cloud credentials lead to customer data exposure"), each with a business risk owner, a 5×5 likelihood and impact rating and a treatment decision.

    Why: Engineers and managers can discuss scenarios. A 300-line asset register would be filled in once and never read again.

    Trade-off: Asset information still has to exist for some controls, so it is kept in the tools the company already uses rather than in a separate spreadsheet.

  3. Justify every Annex A decision from the risk assessment

    Decision: All 93 Annex A controls of ISO/IEC 27001:2022 are considered in the Statement of Applicability. Each one is linked to a risk, a contract or a legal requirement, or has a written reason for exclusion.

    Why: Auditors challenge the reasons behind the Statement of Applicability, not the number of controls. A risk-driven SoA is also easier to defend to customers.

    Trade-off: Writing real justifications takes longer than ticking boxes, but it saves time in Stage 1 and in every customer questionnaire after that.

  4. Collect evidence where the work already happens

    Decision: Change management evidence comes from pull request reviews, access reviews from the identity provider, and vulnerability management from existing ticket workflows. Policies describe how the team already works, after small improvements.

    Why: Evidence that is a by-product of daily work stays current after certification. Separate compliance tasks are the first thing to be dropped when the team is busy.

    Trade-off: Some evidence is less polished than a template would be, so the internal audit checks that it is complete and traceable.

  5. Run a real internal audit and management review before Stage 1

    Decision: An internal audit against all clauses and a sample of Annex A controls, followed by a management review with recorded decisions.

    Why: It finds gaps while there is still time to fix them, and it shows the certification body that the management system works, not only that documents exist.

    Trade-off: It costs about three weeks before the certification audit, which has to be planned into the 12 months from the start.

An exclusion is a decision with a review trigger, not a permanent answer.

04 · What gets delivered

Deliverables

  • ISMS scope statement written for auditors and for customers
  • Risk methodology, risk register and risk treatment plan with named owners
  • Statement of Applicability covering all 93 Annex A controls
  • A core set of policies and procedures that match how the team works
  • Evidence map showing where each control's evidence lives
  • Internal audit report, management review minutes and corrective actions

05 · After certification

What I would keep watching

  • Evidence drift: controls that worked during the audit quietly stop producing evidence. A quarterly evidence check prevents surprises at the surveillance audit.
  • Key-person dependency: security knowledge still sits with a few engineers. Documented runbooks and a deputy for each control owner reduce this.
  • Supplier assurance: new SaaS tools appear quickly. The onboarding process has to catch them before they hold customer data.

The goal is not a certificate. It is a management system the company can still run, and prove, a year later.
